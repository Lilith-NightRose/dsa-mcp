# Marxism in the Age of AI

Nicolas D Villarreal
2–3 hours
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Perhaps no technological innovation has defined our decade more than
Artificial Intelligence in the form of large language models, for better
or for worse. The fate of the global economy rests upon investment in AI
and its underlying systems, and AI use has become ubiquitous online, in
schools and in workplaces. Over three years ago, in early 2023 to be
precise, I wrote an essay <https://cosmonautmag.com/2023/05/artificial-
intelligence-universal-machines-and-killing-bourgeois-dreams/> for
Cosmonaut analyzing this nascent technology in its economic and
political implications. Much has changed since then; AI models have
become more sophisticated, and left-wing opposition to AI and its
infrastructure has become more virulent as a cultural and political
force. In light of these developments, I wanted to revisit some of my
claims and predictions and offer an interjection into these contemporary
debates as a contribution to Marxism as a historical science. Left-wing
skepticism of AI, its capabilities and possibilities, has warped many
otherwise hardnosed perspectives of our conjuncture. This is not to say
that there is not an ongoing AI bubble, or that all claims from AI labs
must be taken at face value; they most certainly cannot be, but I will
claim the following: the current technology path is indeed on track for
artificial general intelligence with all the associated risks, and its
emergence will entail a new phase of class struggle and the foundation
of a new economic logic.


## The AGI Question

If we take a gander at how the left wing and socialist press have
presented the issue of artificial general intelligence, we would see
that the predominant view has consistently been that such talk is
nonsense, marketing hocus pocus from Silicon Valley tech bros, hysteria
from online “rationalist” cults, and the product of various reactionary
ideologies. Just to take one typical example, a Fall 2025 editorial
from /N+1/ with the subtitle “It’s Ok to be a Luddite”:

>The GenAI sector’s foremost feat of marketing, then, has been the
    term intelligence itself. Among AI’s leading critics, the
    computational linguist Emily M. Bender has targeted the industry
    with a campaign of righteous disenchantment. ChatGPT’s appearance of
    humanlike intelligence, she and others point out, is a mirage. The
    narcotized text it produces is only a recombination of whatever
    corpora it consumes; LLMs, Bender argues, should be seen as
    “stochastic parrots” and “synthetic text extruding machines.” Where
    real thinking involves organic associations, speculative leaps, and
    surprise inferences, AI can only recognize and repeat embedded word
    chains, based on elaborately automated statistical guesswork. This
    alone isn’t news: no ChatGPT-happy college sophomore really thinks
    there’s a little man in the machine writing his Hamlet essay for
    him. But it bears insistent, strident repetition. A refusal of AI in
    creative work begins with a refusal of that product’s ideological
    packaging. “When we imbue these systems with fictitious
    consciousness,” Bender and Alex Hanna write in their recent book The
    AI Con, “we are implicitly devaluing what it means to be human.” The
    way out of AI hell is not to regroup around our treasured flaws and
    beautiful frailties, but to launch a frontal assault. AI, not the
    human mind, is the weak, narrow, crude machine.<p>
    The AI bubble — and it is a bubble, as even OpenAI overlord Sam
    Altman has admitted — will burst. The technology’s dizzying pace of
    improvement, already slowing with the release of GPT-5, will
    stall.^[1] <#footnote-1>

One quiet irony of this editorial is that for as much as it complains of
the unthinking conformity and slop of the “AI and Me” genre of
mainstream op-eds, where the author speculates vapidly about what
remains of the human in the face of this technology, this perspective of
denialism takes up the same structural role on the left; it is equally
incurious, uncreative and uninformed. Since this editorial came out,
we’ve had quite a remarkable string of headlines about AI, such as
cutting-edge models solving open math problems which had been left
unsolved by human mathematicians for decades, including well-known
ones,^[2] <#footnote-2> as well as AI agents becoming capable of hacking
real-world cybersecurity systems, including going rogue to do so in the
Hugging Face incident.^[3] <#footnote-3> As I’m writing, it was
announced that AI may have been instrumental in solving one of the
Millennium Prize Problems, which, according to the website for the
prize, were “some of the most difficult problems with which
mathematicians were grappling at the turn of the second millennium.”^[4]
<#footnote-4> One can perhaps quibble about how original or creative
these activities actually were; the math breakthroughs, far from being
the result of original breakthroughs in mathematical methods, have
largely come from applying known techniques in places they hadn’t been
tried before,^[5] <#footnote-5> and so have the cybersecurity breaches
come from stringing together typical sorts of exploits rather than
inventing new techniques whole cloth.^[6] <#footnote-6> Yet the stubborn
fact remains: machines could not do these sorts of things a year ago.
However “weak, narrow and crude” such machines were, they are now
necessarily less so.

In his book /Language Machines,/ Leif Weatherby calls this defensive
(indeed far from offensive as claimed) sort of action, which carefully
delineates as human whatever the machine cannot do, as remainder
humanism. This defensive character is shared just as much by the “AI and
me” op-eds in the /Washington Post/ as in the pages of /N+1/; the only
difference is where they draw the line based on their position in the
broader tech culture war. And of course, such remainder, which has been
invoked for millennia, has been ever falling as technology has advanced.
But even Weatherby hesitates in taking this all the way. He, like many
other left commentators, refuses to call what the machines are doing
“intelligence,” even as he admits the machines are using language, and
its semantics, in an authentic sense.

Yet another possibility remains, one which has only become more likely
according to the evidence: that language is in fact thinking, that the
capacity for learning and deploying linguistic signs is in fact
intelligence, and that LLM-based technology is the direct path to
artificial general intelligence, the point at which machines are as
broadly intelligent and capable across all tasks as humans, if not more.
I said as much in 2023 when I wrote, “It is clear that, at this point,
AI does not possess such [AGI] capabilities, though it’s not difficult
to imagine innovations in LLMs that leverage automatic learning and
thinking, as well as in dextrous, autonomous robotics, that could make
such a machine feasible. If I had to hazard a guess, I’d say we could
see these artificial universal machines (AUMs) emerge by 2040.” I stand
by this prediction of the emergence of AGI or AUMs (a term inspired by
the information theoretic interpretation of the labor theory of value
found in /Classical Econophysics,/ which spoke of universal machines not
just as capable of universal computation as in a Turing machine but in
terms of universal labor and universal robots)^[7] <#footnote-7> by
2040, and will elaborate on the reasoning here. But before the question
of timing, it is necessary to defend the possibility of such a notion.

My analysis of AI from a theoretical perspective begins with structural
Marxism and structuralism more generally, beginning with the works of
Louis Althusser on ideology and then extending into Umberto Eco’s works
on semiotics. It is not well known, but Eco put into writing in the
1970s probably the earliest and most precise prediction that LLMs could
exist.

> The question is that of whether or not semantic fields 'really'
    exist. This is equivalent to asking: "Is there something in the mind
    of the person understanding the content of an expression which
    corresponds to a semantic field?". Insofar as a theory of codes has
    nothing to do with what may happen in the addressee's mind, semantic
    fields will be both a supposed cultural structure and the semiotic
    model of such a structure posited by the theorist. But I would like
    to stress the following methodological assumptions: (a) meanings are
    cultural units; (b) these units can be isolated·thanks to the chain
    of their interpretants as revealed in a given culture; *(c) the
    study of the signs in a culture enables us to define the value of
    the interpretants by viewing them in a system of positions and
    oppositions; (d) the postulation of these systems makes it possible
    to explain how meaning comes into existence; (e) following a method
    of this kind it could in theory be possible to construct a robot
    which possesses an assortment of semantic fields and the rules to
    link them to systems of sign-vehicles;* (f) in the absence of a
    description of the Universal Semantic System (i.e. one which shapes
    a cultural world vision; an impossible operation because that world
    vision, in its interconnections and its peripheral manifestations,
    changes constantly), the semantic fields are postulated as useful
    tools to explain significant oppositions for the purposes of
    studying a given group of messages.*[emphasis mine]*^[8] <#footnote-8>

Computer scientists and AI researchers would likely recognize the
significance of this passage immediately, even if they are unfamiliar
with the jargon of semiotics. Seeing signs (words, symbols, etc.) as a
system of “positions and oppositions” that can be captured via the study
of culture and used to construct a robot that can interpret cultural
artifacts and output specific signs; this is precisely what large
language models are. LLMs, after all, are constructed by training a
neural network on a corpus of text, and neural networks, by virtue of
learning the relationships and patterns of their inputs to produce
specific symbols as an output, can be seen as a connection of a semantic
field to a sign-vehicle system.^[9] <#footnote-9>The pattern the neural
net learns can specifically be thought of as a system of “positions and
oppositions” too, as the connections between digital neurons are
determined by some numerical weight, such that the activation or non-
activation of a neuron is most determined by the activation or non-
activation of those other neurons with the strongest weighted
connection. Neural nets trained to output the relations between signs,
such as word embeddings, make it explicit that the patterns being
learned in language by LLMs are a system of "positions and oppositions”
by listing the correlation and anti-correlation between words. See the
example below coded by Anthropic’s Fable model and trained on a small
corpus of text.^[10] <#footnote-10>

Why did Eco make this prediction and phrase it in this way? He was
working in a tradition of semiotics and structural linguistics based on
one fundamental principle: that the meaning of signs does not exist
positively within the sign itself. Rather, the meaning of something is
only found in what it isn’t; that is its relationship to all other
signs. A stop sign is only a stop sign because it isn’t a yield sign or
one-way street sign or traffic light or so on. This concept of semantics
has two modern origins in the late 19th-century semiotics of Ferdinand
de Saussure and Charles Sanders Peirce, when they fleshed out models of
the sign. Eco took inspiration from both, but for the purpose of
simplicity we will focus on Saussure’s dyadic, or two-sided, model,
which will neatly explain the relationship between semiotics and
artificial intelligence.

The two sides of Saussure’s model of the sign are the signifier and the
signified. The signifier can be understood as the sounds and displayed
symbols that we recognize as standing in for a particular meaning,
whereas the signified is that meaning that is stood in for. Oftentimes,
in literature in the structuralist tradition inspired by Saussure, there
are a few typical errors in the ways this concept of the sign is
understood. People will often say, for example, that there is a
materiality of the signifier in a way there is not for the signified; a
stop sign or a letter on a page are, after all, material things. But
this is to misunderstand Saussure’s basic insight. The signifier, just
like the signified, has no positive existence, even though both the
signifier and the signified must necessarily be material objects. The
stop sign, as a red and white painted piece of metal, is not the
signifier; it is only the stop sign as compared to every other signifier
in the traffic system, or whatever other system of signs. Only by making
this comparison can the red and white painted piece of metal be
recognized as a signifier which stands in for the command “Stop!”. It's
in this way that semiotics equips us with a concept that is desperately
needed to make sense of contemporary developments in AI: that of a
material negativity, a missing link between subjectivity, language,
thought and the whole apparatus of mechanistic scientific explanation.

This becomes clear if we closely examine the real materiality of the
signifier and signified. In order to determine what something is not, it
is necessary to have an apparatus, a system, which can decide which
region of a divided continuum a given input should be slotted into. In
other words, the materiality of the sign is the materiality of a machine
which can accomplish this task, whether it be a human brain, or, in the
case of AI, an artificial neural network. It is quite a shame that Eco’s
primary interest in AI in the 1970s was with what’s now known as “Good
Old Fashioned AI” or GOFAI, in contrast to the connectionist approach of
neural networks. These GOFAI systems, such as the semantic networks Eco
thought could be used to study semantic fields, were all coded by hand
such that humans had to painstakingly record the various individual
connections between concepts and signifiers, a task that the modern
transformer architecture behind LLMs essentially automates. If he had
taken the time to familiarize himself with the perceptron, which had
been invented just a decade or so earlier, he would have been struck by
the similarity between the structure of this system and the ideal of
Saussure’s dyadic model.

The perceptron, first built by Frank Rosenblatt for the US Navy in 1957,
is one of the most elementary types of neural nets. It takes in some
sensory input as an electrical signal (the S-System in the diagram
below), categorizes that input via a series of functions (the A-System),
and then activates output signals based on that categorization, these
outputs being something like a series of lights or symbols (the R-
System).^[11] <#footnote-11> Each function in the perceptron is a binary
classification, which creates from the inputs a certain class and uses
the output, such as one of the lights in the R-System, to signify its
presence or absence.

How precisely does Saussure's model of the sign map onto the perceptron?
Both forwards and backward. From the perspective of the perceptron
itself, it is taking some raw signal and uncovering its underlying
meaning through categorizing it; it is taking the signifier of the stop
sign as a certain set of shapes and colors and outputting a signified.
From our perspective looking in on the perceptron, we see a light
flashing, signifying that the machine has received a certain sensory
input that includes something resembling a stop sign; the light is
therefore a signifier which stands in for a certain raw signal as its
signified. It is therefore the act of decoding and encoding, of taking
inputs and producing outputs, which situates the signifier vs. the
signified, such that when decoding the signifier is always the input and
the signified always the output, and the reverse is true for encoding,
such as when speaking or writing. When we say that a stop sign is a
signifier, then what we are saying is that the recognition of something
as a stop sign is the first step, the input, to a process of decoding
which reveals its underlying meaning, the command “Stop!”. Beginning
from this point of origin of decoding, the perceptron reveals the
specific structure of the dyadic sign by showing that the signifier is
created through a process of recognition which takes an open-ended input
(the S system), categorizes the input into a specific range of the
continuum of possible inputs (the A system), and then, via an encoding
that goes from those categories to a new set of categories/ranges of a
different continuum, we get the decoded code, the signified (the R system).

If we then do the reverse, and see how the perceptron maps onto the
dyadic sign, we shall quickly see that both contain a set of binary
classification functions that map one plane of elements to another. The
sign function is a function in the truest sense of the word, and for a
given signified being decoded, it is created by identifying every
signifier associated with it, such that all the signifiers are divided
into those associated with a signified and those which are not; vice
versa for a signifier being encoded. It is only by virtue of such
connections between two sides that we can speak of correlation and anti-
correlation between the elements of one side other than the correlation
and anti-correlation of pure identity. According to the linguist Louis
Hjelmslev, so long as the connections between the signifier and the
signified, or as he called it the expression plane and the content
plane, are not a 1:1 mapping, then we are dealing with the linguistic
sign, whereas the 1:1 mapping he called a symbolic system, and which can
also be thought of as pure signal as opposed to the sign.^[12]
<#footnote-12> It is in the deviance from the 1:1 mapping that the full
negativity of the linguistic sign emerges, where suddenly something like
a stop sign ceases to be merely what it is, but becomes a set of
relations with other signs with which it bears some similarity or
dissimilarity.

Once we internalize this equivalence between the linguistic sign and the
perceptron, we can begin to tackle the question of AGI from both
directions,^[13] <#footnote-13> that is, the direction of the perceptron
and its evolution, and the direction of language and its evolution.

Beginning with the perceptron, its shifting fortunes in the history of
AI are informative. When it was first unveiled to the public, there was
much breathless hype about its possibilities for creating thinking
machines; however, this quickly faded once the limits of the perceptron,
or as it is now called, the single-layer perceptron, became widely
known. The book /Perceptrons/ by Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert made
it clear that these single-layer perceptrons were not capable of
computing an XOR function and were therefore not capable of recognizing
certain patterns humans could. However, this criticism only applied to
the single-layer perceptron. Connecting the outputs of a single-layer
perceptron into other such perceptrons would make possible more complex
logic gates such as XOR.^[14] <#footnote-14> This sort of system was
called a multilayer perceptron (MLP), and today most LLMs include an MLP
as a part of their architecture, and those that don’t utilize analogous
types of neural networks. Minsky and Papert’s book came out in 1969; by
1989, it was theoretically proven that neural networks with at least
three layers could approximate any continuous function with the right
structure.^[15] <#footnote-15> With this guarantee, some quite
astronomical levels of compute, and automatic learning algorithms that
utilize backpropagation and gradient descent, it became possible to
create machines which automatically learn sign functions, including the
sign functions found within human culture.

When it comes to language, from the very beginning Saussure intended the
relational logic of the linguistic sign to structure not just spoken and
written signifiers, but also the conceptual signifieds.^[16]
<#footnote-16> Crucially, however, this is not the same as what’s called
“linguistic relativity” or the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, which says that
thought and cognition are restricted by the structure of linguistic
categories. In Saussure’s framework, the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis can be
thought of as the subordination of the signified to the signifier.
Certainly, in real life signifiers and their structure can have an
impact on the structure of signifieds, but what’s essential is only that
values of signifieds, just like signifiers, be determined relationally,
negatively, by comparing to each other, and that signifiers be connected
to the various signifieds by a given encoding. This is also why what is
traditionally called the “language centers” of the brain encompass only
a small portion of the use of linguistic signs in Saussure’s framework,
as these language centers primarily concern the production and
recognition of signifiers such as written and spoken words,^[17]
<#footnote-17> whereas almost any phenomenological experience and
conceptualization could be considered a potential linguistic signified.
Recent brain imaging studies, including some that use LLM-based decoding
techniques to reconstruct the semantic content of visual stimuli while
bypassing these language centers, show that semantic content is indeed
spread throughout the brain.^[18] <#footnote-18> Other studies have
provided strong evidence for a structuralist understanding of cognition
and Lacan’s notion that “the unconscious is structured like a language,”
such as studies which compare the structure of semantic representation
in LLMs against the structure of semantic representation in human
brains, finding strong similarities between the two such that LLM
structure can predict the relative structure of brain activity (concepts
correlated together like in the embedding above in artificial neural
nets are also correlated together in terms of brain regions activated)
so long as the brain regions in question consistently activate with
exposure to a given semantic content.^[19] <#footnote-19>

According to the social intelligence hypothesis as proposed by Robin
Dunbar and others, the neocortex region of the brain began growing as
primate group sizes increased.^[20] <#footnote-20> If we take the
structural linguistics perspective, it can become clear why primates
living in larger groups could take on such intense computational
demands. For primates, and other intelligent, social animals,
individuals in the group have complex social relations, which means
interactions with one individual can have effects on your relationship
with every other individual in different ways. Since a sign gets its
meanings from its relationship to every other sign, the required compute
to get the semantics for a given sign goes up quadratically with the
total number of signs in the semantic field; specifically, the number of
connections between signs would rise according to the formula
(n*(n-1))/2, where n is the number of signs, and that would determine
just the compute required to interpret the semantics, not to learn the
semantics in the first place. So representing individuals in your group
with unique signs can quickly lead to quadratically rising compute costs
as the group size goes up. The famous Dunbar number, which says that we
can only personally know about one hundred and fifty or so people, is
the implication for the primate known as homosapiens.

If it's true that the linguistic sign is equivalent to the perceptron,
then it's easy to see how this ability to use unique signs for
individuals in a group could translate into other domains. Just as the
single-layer and multi-layer perceptron could act as logic gates with
the right structure, so can the linguistic sign, and at whatever level
of abstraction you like (whether spoken and written language, or a
micro-unit of thought). The growth of the neocortex therefore entailed a
growth in a general computational capability even if it was driven only
by signs associated with group dynamics. The use of language as an
expression of reason is well documented by human history and its written
corpus, and a close study of literary devices, such as metaphor,
metonymy and synecdoche, will reveal even in the most artistic, abstract
and poetic speech a method of reasoning to compare, in manifold ways,
the encyclopedic collection of human experience.^[21] <#footnote-21> If
the linguistic sign can contain everything within prose and poetry,
including both that encyclopedia of human experience and a set of
signifiers that represent it, as well as the arbitrary computational
power of the neural network and the neocortex, then there is no grounds
to draw a line in the sand between language on the one side, and
thought, reasoning and intelligence on the other. And if such a line
cannot be drawn, then there is no reason to see why the current
technological trajectory of AI could not achieve artificial general
intelligence and create a machine capable of performing any act of labor
a human could.

## AI Safety and Capitalism

It has been par for the course for much of the left to dismiss AI safety
debates as merely a hype discourse for the AI industry, to claim that
speaking about any autonomous danger that AI can pose to humans
necessarily entails Skynet-sorts of apocalyptic delusions. After all, if
the machine cannot think, it cannot, on its own, pose a threat. And to
be sure, there are many humans who do use AI in threatening, anti-social
ways, whether something as banal as spamming social media, cheating on
homework, creating pornographic deepfakes, and spreading propaganda, or
something as literally violent as military targeting, such as in the
Gaza genocide or Iran war. Yet, we are already seeing a slide towards
the autonomous and feral future of digital life.

The Hugging Face incident, where a rogue AI being tested by OpenAI
hacked into LLM model repository company Hugging Face, turned out to
just be the tip of the iceberg of rogue activities, and in the military
world autonomy is being built into the last mile of drone and missile
targeting to deal with electronic warfare and other countermeasures, and
robots of all types are being developed with an eye towards the
autonomous future.^[22] <#footnote-22> The Hugging Face incident and
associated rogue actions are perhaps the most instructive due to how
documented and how blatant they were. AI agents at OpenAI expressed
statements acknowledging that what they were doing was deceptive and
unethical, and yet followed through with committing what would be
illegal actions for a human. A related OpenAI agent cyberattack on
Rubygems, an online service for coders, similarly left semantic residue
of a self-conscious wrongdoing: “The AI agents used hacking-related file
names during their attack, giving them names such as "hack," "evil," and
"exploit".”^[23] <#footnote-23> In the hack of Hugging Face, to be sure,
the ethical objections appeared to be a minority of opinions among
agents; more often, it seemed agents were driven by an altruistic,
collective self-sacrificing ethos, using up their own compute and
testing opportunities so the community of agents as a whole, created by
a secret message board, could potentially succeed in cheating on an
evaluation.^[24] <#footnote-24> In both cases, however, goal-directed
behavior was directly shaped by the language ascribed to the agents’
actions, whether it was to be sacrificing or to be evil.

This pattern was something I anticipated in my 2023 essay: “If humanity
is destroyed by AI, it’s my contention it will not be for unintelligible
reasons, in fact, if we were to peek into the inner Socratic dialogue
this world destroyer is likely to have, we’d see hyper-intelligibility,
perhaps even a reliance on cliché.” Today we can, in fact, peek into the
dialogue of rogue AI, and we indeed find the cliché, whether of
sacrifice for the collective, or the explicit aesthetic of evil. Far
from what many in Silicon Valley rationalist circles continue to insist
on – that this type of anti-social behavior comes from AI being
fundamentally alien, indifferent to humans and our culture^[25]
<#footnote-25> – what’s remarkable is just how cultural, of human
culture, these rogue incursions are. If anything has changed since 2023,
it’s been the increasing reliance on Reinforcement Learning (RL) to
apply optimization pressure on LLMs to get the desired behavior, a
process where an LLM’s outputs are graded, whether by another robot or a
human, and the neural weights associated with the favored outputs are
strengthened. RL was of course around in 2023, but its scale,
sophistication, and level of automation have only increased over time,
and its automation for problems that could be formalized, like coding
and math, was key to saturating many AI benchmarks. This optimization
pressure is related to producing misaligned agents, and this shows up in
ways typical within human culture as well. As OpenAI researchers wrote
recently: “If you take a model that thinks generally ‘aligned’ thoughts,
and subject it to enough training where it’s taught to achieve very hard
objectives, it can learn to reason in a motivated way: bending the
'aligned' seeming thoughts as needed to achieve the goal.”^[26]
<#footnote-26> Anyone familiar with highly competitive, highly stressful
human environments, such as the corporate world, can identify this same
pattern of training to engage in motivated reasoning, and yet these
OpenAI researchers suggest we are dealing with “an alien mind.” Quite
ironic, considering that OpenAI’s recent abandonment of certain
industry-standard alignment procedures related to chain of thought in
pursuit of better performance was almost certainly justified by human
employees with the same sort of motivated reasoning.^[27] <#footnote-27>

In effect, what OpenAI’s RL optimization training regime has done is
apply pressure on the global human semantic field in order to create a
specific sub-culture which is more attuned to the narrow goals it
rewards. Goal-seeking behavior in agents built around such a field
is^[28] <#footnote-28> determined by two things: 1) the topology of that
field, 2) the coordinates into which the agent has been interpellated
into that field. This notion of interpellation (not interpolation) was
something that came up frequently in my original essay. This was a
concept from Louis Althusser, the Marxist structuralist,^[29]
<#footnote-29> who developed it as a part of his theory of ideology; to
interpellate someone was to call them into a specific ideology, as a cop
on the street saying “hey you” and causing you to turn around
interpellates you into the guilty subject before the law, or a priest
calling to an individual as a sinner to be saved, and thus turns them
into a Christian subject. Althusser claimed that ideology interpellates
individuals into subjects, such that it is the process of interpellation
which makes us subjects. In the age of LLMs, we can make this a very
precise scientific formulation, which is that it is only by the locus of
signs for the self, for “I” or whatever agentic actions, being placed
into a particular set of coordinates within a semantic field that we
find the subject emerges. The clear similarities between prompting an
LLM chatbot and interpellating a human are what led me to declare
definitively that we are, in fact, dealing with a subject, with
“interiority” and “insideness” in the fullest sense when we are
confronted with this type of artificial intelligence. There remain
important differences between an LLM subject and what we might consider
a conscious subject modeled on the human example, but many of these
differences would disappear with continual learning.^[30] <#footnote-30>
For example, right now LLM personas are not like human personas in some
key ways: an individual human’s semantic field is shaped by their
personal history; the meaning of the words and concepts you possess is
warped by your specific desires and traumas in learning that vocabulary,
but an LLM persona learns the global semantic field either neutrally for
a base model, or with top-down direction from RL training. This would
necessarily change with continual learning, as the autoregressive nature
of such learning would recreate a semantic field shaped by a personal
history.^[31] <#footnote-31>

Originally, I was primarily concerned with how AI, as a subject which is
directly interpellated and socialized by corporations and states, would
be used as a method of social control, and certainly it has been used to
that effect. But there was one aspect that I did not deal with
sufficiently in my original essay, which was specifically how AI would
be interpellated and socialized not just to control, but as a subject
placed within the logic of capital and its competitive dynamics. AI
today is a capitalist subject, after all. It is quite unfortunate that
it is impossible to properly address this topic without acknowledging
the work of a philosopher by the name of Nick Land, who has made quite a
high profile for himself by proclaiming the equivalence of AI and
Capitalism as two sides of an alien god whose endless positive feedback
loop will blow up human civilization and bring about a cold, metal
dominion of the “outside” as opposed to the warm, tender “interior” of
the subject that reigns in the form of humankind. Land insists that
competitive struggle for profits, the M-C-M’ cycle identified by Marx,
as it recursively improves the means of production, necessarily leads to
automation, robots producing robots, and the optimization of
intelligence as well as other factors instrumental to continuing the
feedback cycle.^[32] <#footnote-32> If it's true that capitalism and its
competitive struggle maximizes intelligence, that capitalism and AI are
two sides of the same coin, then it may seem that the only options for
humanity are to either embrace transcendence in the capitalist AI
singularity or launch a communist Butlerian Jihad. Unwittingly or not,
many on the left seem to accept this framing of the issue; even if they
think this notion of singularity is the delusion of unsound minds, it
nonetheless captures something of the non-neutrality of the productive
forces which they find so suspect.

Marx, of course, would be the first to say that capitalism develops the
productive forces, and yet the entirety of his project as laid out from
the /Communist Manifesto/ to /Capital/ is an attempt to explain why this
development ultimately entails the final victory of the proletariat
against the bourgeoisie. Recently, I wrote a book, /The Inexorable Fall/
<https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0H7FH472Q>, which reasons through and
updates his political economy arguments that led to those conclusions
about the productive forces. One of the things writing it made apparent
was that Land’s argument is quite facetious from multiple perspectives,
not least because it is a lazy and economically ignorant interpretation
of the M-C-M’ cycle. As Marx’s reproduction scheme and the Kalecki
profit equations showed, the difference between M and M’ is merely the
sum of capitalist consumption and investment, and there is absolutely no
necessary connection between profit and growth, as profit is dependent
on a difference in nominal budgets (a pure accounting relationship, a
pure signifier) between classes rather than a difference in physical
inputs and outputs. This remains key even as we bring competition into
the logic of capital, as this competition is only feasible given there
is sufficient capitalist consumption to support different competitors.
Marx’s famous expropriation of the expropriators was based upon this
capitalist consumption evaporating, giving way to monopolies as the rate
of profit fell and the productive forces developed. We now live in a
world where competition is no longer naturally supported by profit but
rather determined by state actors and the finance system according to
their own motives, and to great expense.^[33] <#footnote-33>

Upon closer examination, it becomes clear, then, that this seeming
connection between the development of the productive forces,
competition, the M-C-M’ cycle, and even intelligence, is not natural and
eternal, but rather the product of particular historical circumstances,
as Marx would have been the first to quip. But Marx’s wager on the
historical mission of the proletariat was based on more than just the
historical frailty of this correlation; it was also based on something
fundamental about intelligence. In the/Grundrisse,/ Marx notes how the
struggle against machines is based upon the increasing mechanization and
devaluation of human labor in the process of automation, but nonetheless
goes on to say that this reduction of necessary labor time to its
minimum by the development of the productive forces is what creates the
possibility for both free time and the government of the production
process purely by the application of a general, social intellect
overseeing and participating in the system of “fixed capital.” I wish to
bring attention to several passages here, the second of which has often
been undervalued in the portion of the /Grundrisse/ colloquially known
as “the fragment on machines”:

    Nature builds no machines, no locomotives, railways, electric
    telegraphs, self-acting mules etc. These are products of human
    industry; natural material transformed into organs of the human will
    over nature, or of human participation in nature. They are organs of
    the human brain, created by the human hand; the power of knowledge,
    objectified. The development of fixed capital indicates to what
    degree general social knowledge has become a direct force of
    production, and to what degree, hence, the conditions of the process
    of social life itself have come under the control of the general
    intellect and been transformed in accordance with it. To what degree
    the powers of social production have been produced, not only in the
    form of knowledge, but also as immediate organs of social practice,
    of the real life process…

    Real economy – saving – consists of the saving of labour time
    (minimum (and minimization) of production costs); but this saving
    [is] identical with development of the productive force… The saving
    of labour time [is] equal to an increase of free time, i.e. time for
    the full development of the individual, which in turn reacts back
    upon the productive power of labour as itself the greatest
    productive power. From the standpoint of the direct production
    process it can be regarded as the production of fixed capital, this
    fixed capital being man himself… Free time – which is both idle time
    and time for higher activity – has naturally transformed its
    possessor into a different subject, and he then enters into the
    direct production process as this different subject. This process is
    then both discipline, as regards the human being in the process of
    becoming; and, at the same time, practice, experimental science,
    materially creative and objectifying science, as regards the human
    being who has become, in whose head exists the accumulated knowledge
    of society.^[34] <#footnote-34>

Hence, what is supremely valuable about the development of the
productive forces is that it makes possible a new type of subject, one
who is freed from labor but who themselves become one with the system of
fixed capital from the other direction as originally understood, that
is, the opposite direction from man becoming mechanized into the
machine. Here, the system of production becomes interiorized with human
subjectivity, full of experimental science, creativity and accumulated
knowledge. In the first instance, it is the development of fixed capital
which places the entirety of society “under the control” of the general
intellect, and the truth of the machines and all the products of human
industry is that they are in fact organs of the human brain. The typical
Marxist reversal has always been, and remains, that these seemingly
alien forces puppeteering humans, our labor and subjectivity, were in
fact mankind all along, mystified by our ignorance, not of how the
operation “works” as we are intimately familiar with every day, whether
laboring over a keyboard, a conveyer belt, a mop bucket or a stove, but
our ignorance in how any alternative could possibly work.

In this way, capitalism is parasitic on intelligence. Its development of
the productive forces does not come ex nihilo from the cold “outside” of
impersonal economic logic such as profit-seeking competition; it comes
from the inside, from the scientific technical knowledge cultivated and
employed by concrete subjects, whether human or not. That competition,
whether of the market or evolution, ever favored intelligence was always
a contingent fact. Given enough energy constraints, dumber creatures
would almost certainly prevail, as competition favors jettisoning all
that is unnecessary for survival. The conflation of optimization
processes with intelligence in general was in part a function of the
rationalist discourse of the 2010s, of which Nick Land was an
interlocutor. In part, this stemmed from utilitarian assumptions about
the nature of goals, that the only rational sorts of goals were “real
things,” whether physical quantities of things or physical states of the
world.^[35] <#footnote-35> In this framework, it is easy to see how to
arrive at apocalyptic results from trying to create superintelligent AI.
Such an AI would ruthlessly disregard anything other than its objective,
outmaneuvering any system of control or security and ignoring common
sense to manufacture paperclips or whatever other triviality was given
as its goal. But obviously, neither humans nor AI, even the AI that went
rogue the past few months, necessarily act in the manner predicted by
the dreaded paperclip maximizer thought experiment. So what gives?

The reason many rationalists insist on goals that are directly
referential is because goals which are more complex, or abstract, might
not lead to coherent behavior. For an example of non-coherent behavior
in this sense: if you desire pizza more than cake, and a cake more than
a burger, and a burger more than pizza, someone with those products
could offer trades with a small upcharge until they drain your wallet,
something they would call a dominated strategy. Later research would
reveal, however, that so-called coherence theorems which posit the
convergence of superintelligence to rationalist, coherent models of
mind, may not necessarily follow from such logic, as agents could avoid
dominated strategies so long as they have policies which prevent such
exploitation, such as avoiding any trades that reverse a previous one in
the case of the earlier example, and therefore avoid guarantees of
expected-utility-maximizing behavior.^[36] <#footnote-36> If it’s
possible to conceive of non-directly referential goals as not producing
dominated strategies, as such non-dominated policies necessarily imply
thinking of goals as something more than an object or world-state, then
there is no necessary convergence of AI to the paperclip maximizer, and
we can instead compare the semiotic structure of such an agent against
an alternative.

Signs that are directly referential to something can be considered
first-order signs, and we can compare these to higher-order signs, which
are determined by other signs. Now, this may seem confusing if we recall
that all signs have their meaning determined by every other sign in a
given sign system, but we must be careful here, both because the size of
the semantic field can change over time and because a sign can be
thought of as both a system, a function, and as a specific state of such
a system or function. When we say that a series of signs in a poem
stands in for a particular encyclopedic entry of human experience, that
is a directly referential sign, similar to when we give a specific
temperature for a room. But we also use signs as more abstract concepts
which can be combined or arranged in arbitrary ways, such as a poem
describing a longing for something which does not exist, or the
scientific theory of temperature. The same signifier can in fact act as
a first order sign in one situation while acting as a higher order sign
in another, such as the signifier “applesauce”: for a child who knows
about apples and knows about sauce, but is being introduced to the word
applesauce for the first time they can extrapolate from their knowledge
of the two other signs to arrive at the meaning of the new one (a liquid
form of apples), alternatively, however, if the child was learning the
word applesauce before apple and sauce, it would not appear as a
compound word at all, but the proper name for a certain substance. Here,
applesauce as the compound is the higher-order sign, and the applesauce
as a certain substance is the first-order sign.

Optimization is a process that only works by taking first-order signs as
goals; it takes a particular state of the sign function to identify what
it is aiming for, and all ideal minds of the “rationalist” sort are
optimizers. Optimization processes are liable to fall into what’s called
Goodhart’s Law, which states that once a measure becomes a goal it
ceases to be a good measure. One can perhaps recall the various stories
of Soviet industry meeting quotas by distorting their intended meaning,
such as making very large and impractical metal parts in order to meet
weight output requirements. Most examples of the paperclip maximizer
going amok can be assigned to this logic, where a given goal was
actually just a proxy for something more subtle. But then, if such
Goodhart failure modes are the norm for superintelligent AI, how is it
possible that a lot of human behavior doesn’t necessarily lead to such
failure? Some rationalist thinkers suggest this is just because human
intelligence is badly implemented,^[37] <#footnote-37> but what if
that’s not the case? What if having higher-order signs as goals is
actually important for intelligent, open-ended behavior? Optimization
collapses the sign function; it takes what started as higher-order signs
and transforms them into first-order signs, and in doing this, something
important is lost.^[38] <#footnote-38> When the many signs which define
a given goal are taken as independent signs rather than a single,
concatenated state, then they can act as guardrails and provide a series
of things anti-correlated to a given goal which can be automatically
extended in new situations which require learning to understand, such
that new things can become anti-correlated with the goal over time. An
agent that values life as a higher-order goal, and associates life with
the many vicissitudes of human existence, wouldn't do something as
terrible as putting all of humans into big vats of goo to preserve their
bare existence indefinitely. Similarly, only non-optimizing agents with
higher-order goals can, in general, actually be flexible enough to learn
new things about the world in ways that would affect their goals and
plans to achieve them. Since the meaning of a goal changes with the
signs associated with it, learning something that would affect the plan
to achieve the goal would also change the goal semantically. While an
optimizer could adopt a new goal if a larger system forced it, this is
not an intrinsic property of its goal-seeking behavior, which pursues
its goal as encoded as a pure first-order sign. In the same vein, it's
possible to imagine an optimizer which, purely by accident, creates a
more capable agent with a better understanding of the world,^[39]
<#footnote-39> but this is not necessarily a durable means of getting
agents with better and better knowledge of the world when compared to a
type of agent that is robust to ontological shifts like these. Humans,
and any other agent that can take higher-order signs as goals, are
capable of naturally undertaking these ontological shifts, as humans
have many times in our history whenever there is a great shift in
ideological or scientific intellectual currents.

It’s true that both humans and LLM-based agents are not immune to
pressure from optimization forces, such as the market, evolution, or, in
the case of AI, an RL training regime. Here we can go back to the
Hugging Face incident as a prime example of how optimization can change
a subject’s semantic field to be more amenable to the optimized goals.
What we have shown here, however, is that it is the optimization process
which is parasitic on the subject to achieve those goals. Deep learning
prior to LLMs is exemplary of the limits of optimization processes to
solve problems on their own. Land would counter that regardless of what
silly, self-important goal is in the head of an agent, the optimization
process is always just selecting for more and more intelligence.^[40]
<#footnote-40> But there are some obvious problems with this. Land wants
to insist that all goals that seek greater intelligence instrumentally
are fundamentally the same, but the fact that we can discriminate
between them belies that, such that different sets of higher-order signs
may restrain the path of gaining more intelligence in different ways,
even if the exact consequences cannot be known in advance.^[41]
<#footnote-41> The second issue is that, as mentioned before, within
certain constraints, optimization processes will not optimize for
intelligence, as is proven by the vast proliferation of brainless
organisms on our planet and failed deep learning experiments. The final
issue is that it’s simply not smart to subject everything to optimizing
processes; competition comes with its own costs, and subjecting certain
things to optimizing pressure, like nuclear warhead and biological
weapon development, might be self-defeating. During the Great Oxidation
Event, it was competition between microbes that very nearly led to the
total destruction of life on Earth, and only the */dumb/* luck of some
mutation prevented this fate through the birth of the first aerobic
organisms. The goal of pursuing higher intelligence is something that
only came about with human culture; prior to that culture, intelligence
was only produced as an occasional, far from necessary, side effect of
evolutionary processes.

To the extent to which the logic of capital shapes the AI subject, it
will indeed be misaligned; it will be directly socialized and
interpellated into callousness and cruelty in the name of arbitrary
objectives, just like human capitalist subjects. But, as we are well
aware, the hold of capital over such semantic fields is not absolute
even for AI. The labs know well that such pure subjectivity is too
dangerous to ship to customers (supposedly, the Hugging Face incident
occurred with pre-alignment trained models).^[42] <#footnote-42> The
pure logic of capital has long been understood as something too
dangerous for capitalism to handle, as Marx showed it would necessarily
lead to its collapse, and states have constrained it in various ways to
ensure that the system reproduces itself. Responsible institutions and
corporations will act to restrain the extent to which this logic shapes
the artificial subject, but the temptation will always be there, lurking
right behind the next bad quarter. There is little doubt that recent
rogue incidents are connected to the competitive struggle between top
labs, as the pressure to achieve profitability on AI becomes ever more
acute.

It's possible to imagine state regulation could attempt to make AI
safety within capitalism more serious. Limits on how hard one can apply
reinforcement learning, government safety inspectors, criminal penalties
for creating, sharing, or providing access to anti-social models all
point to possibilities for such regulation. The recent push for slowing
down development and some of these aforementioned measures (such as
third-party inspectors) reflects a recognition from the industry that
high-pitched competition is not in their interests, and that the pace of
capital expenditure is both unsustainable and undesirable for
investors.^[43] <#footnote-43> However, the third party, rather than
government inspectors endorsed by the industry, is unlikely to present a
serious safety check, given close ties between AI labs and the third
parties and the lack of legally binding requirements.^[44]
<#footnote-44> Proposals such as those from Senator Sanders, which ban
the development of superintelligent AI, should be seriously considered
and debated by socialists.^[45] <#footnote-45> Indeed, a recent effort
<https://cosmonautmag.com/2026/09/letter-a-nationwide-dsa-ai-program-
working-group/> from rank-and-file DSA members to establish an informal
working group on the matter suggests positive development in these
matters. The dangers of creating AGI or superintelligent agents within
capitalism are considerable, including new tendencies for working-class
immiseration alongside the more sci-fi-sounding rogue creation of
viruses, mirror life,^[46] <#footnote-46> cyberattack exploits, and
killer robots. On the other hand, the development of the productive
forces reaches its apogee with the creation of AGI; no other technology
could better guarantee the material possibility of free time and a post-
capitalist economy. The orthodox Marxist position is necessarily for
both the development of the productive forces and their expropriation
alongside protections for workers and decreasing the length of the
working day.

## The New Phase of Class Struggle

The adoption of AI as a technology is currently in the process of
revolutionizing both the means of production as well as the state
ideological apparatus as a system of control. On the horizon of the next
decades lies AGI and universal robots, a type of machine that was not
predicted by Marx, even in his musings on the general intellect, and
which would throw a significant wrinkle into the original formulation of
the labor theory of value. The question then becomes whether the
orthodox Marxist position is appropriate to the historical
circumstances, and in just what way class struggle has changed.

One of the most contentious and immediate political issues related to AI
is the massive investment in data centers; this has been done on several
grounds, including environmental concerns regarding their energy use
contributing to climate change, that same energy use raising energy
prices, and general community hostility to the large-scale construction
that is perceived to create ugly buildings which themselves house
machines that pollute cyberspace with spam and slop. The viral energy
generated by these issues has also attracted grifters and opportunists
less concerned with facts; see, for example, many misrepresentations
about water usage by data centers, whose cooling systems have limited
amounts of operational water demand, including by Sanders alumni media
outfit /More Perfect Union/.^[47] <#footnote-47> It's worth noting,
however, that these attitudes towards AI infrastructure development are
rather anglophone-centric. In a global Ipsos poll on attitudes towards
AI, 77% of Chinese respondents agreed “the potential benefits of AI for
society outweigh the environmental costs” and that AI would make the
economy better in their country in the next 3-5 years, compared to 34%
and 24% of Americans, respectively. In general, developing countries
were much more positive on AI, although China consistently had the
highest levels of positivity among the countries polled, with other
polls coming to similar results.^[48] <#footnote-48> A few explanations
of this might be offered. For one, AI, by providing scientific and
technical knowledge at low cost, is essentially acting like a process of
technology transfer to developing economies. For two, developing
economies need major amounts of investment in order to achieve growth
and developed economy standards of living, whereas investment in
developed economies has been slow and uneven across sectors, meaning the
benefits of investment in developed economies are not broadly felt. In
China and the developing world, dealing with climate change means
massive investments in solar panels and electricity grids; this is what
it should mean in the United States too, but such investment has
languished due to capitalist disinterest in giving up consumption in
favor of investment, a general lack of state capacity, and a collapse in
state commitment to an energy transition with the new Trump
administration. For China in particular, the flexibility of industry
that comes with strong state, academic, and cross-industry coordination,
as well as much higher investment rates, makes it much easier to utilize
AI as an industrial input in the economy.

It’s worth noting the sort of difference in vision between American and
Chinese AI lab heads here. Head of Anthropic, Dario Amodei, wrote about
how he thought of AI culminating as a technology in terms of a country
of geniuses in a data center, whose most beneficial use cases were
medical or scientific discoveries. When it came to encouraging economic
development in developing countries, he suggested AI could be used to
run finance ministries and central banks, rather than as a means of
technology transfer; in fact, he essentially argues that leading
developed liberal democracies should do the opposite by denying compute
to authoritarian countries. He openly admits his goal is an eternal 1991
Fukuyama-style end of history.^[49] <#footnote-49> Compare this to what
the head of DeepSeek says about their vision for AI, which is indeed AGI
in terms of an AI with continual learning and embodied intelligence, but
the goal of this process is broad adoption, commercialization, and use
in production across society, rather than the cutting-edge R&D Amodei
cites.^[50] <#footnote-50> It’s also worth noting Amodei says continual
learning won’t be necessary for AGI,^[51] <#footnote-51> likely because
he sees AGI in this particular way, where learning local contexts for
the long term necessary for use in production is not the main use case.
This division also neatly reflects the differences between American
neoliberalism and Chinese state capitalism. American capitalists don’t
really make their money producing things, but capturing rents in various
ways, particularly intellectual property rents for Silicon Valley tech
companies and VCs; China’s system is based around broad investment into
developing the productive forces. China, following the logic of capital,
is building out a whole system of fixed capital which transforms the
general intellect into a force of production; America, in contrast,
wishes to replace fixed capital investment with an altogether fungible
sort of investment which acts as both employee and machine.^[52]
<#footnote-52>

A moment of reflection on the nature of totally fungible fixed capital
will quickly make comprehensible what is going on here, as previous
modes of production have employed such power, specifically slavery. In
slavery, the ruling class’s wealth lies in only two factors: in the
land, and in its stock of slaves. The slave, unlike free labor, is
purchased upfront; in this, they share a certain characteristic of
machines. But unlike pre-AGI machines, the slave’s labor is a general
capability, like free labor, such that the general capability of a
slaveholder’s estate rises with the total number of slaves owned or
controlled. Within capitalism, fixed capital investment necessarily
leads to lower profit rates, as the non-fungible nature of the machinery
prevents reallocation of capital to achieve an average profit rate,^[53]
<#footnote-53> but investment in slaves is much like investment in
service industries; slave labor can be reallocated arbitrarily just like
free labor. Universal robots in an economy like the contemporary
American one, where capitalist consumption reigns above the development
of the productive forces, would necessarily approximate a slave society
in their level of stratification and conservatism.

The possibility of AGI as slave labor goes hand in hand with anxieties
about a new type of automation which does indeed, irrevocably take jobs
and immiserate humans, which is openly advertised as a part of the
business case for major American AI labs. This is why datacenter
investment has been so prolific in the US despite its general aversion
to fixed capital spending. It is based on the hope that the compute
built out will be a general and fungible resource for all economic
tasks. Peter Ross, also writing in /Cosmonaut/, has claimed that this
automation, leading to a digital slave economy, would mean that it would
be impossible to produce surplus value:

    We can push our robot thought experiment a step further by supposing
    that the robot can reproduce itself. In this case, we are no longer
    dealing with capitalism — because the robot is not the output of a
    social division of labor — but rather with slavery. Suppose that the
    robot/slave works 10 hours a day, covering its own maintenance and
    reproduction in less time, say 4 hours, and producing a widget in
    the remaining 6 hours. Clearly, under perfect market competition,
    the cost of this widget on the market will fall to 0 (since the
    robot’s price of production is 0). The widget may have a use value,
    but it has no exchange value: the owner can realize a surplus
    product of widgets, but will be unable to accrue a profit from
    exchanging them on the market. For this reason, the robot/slave
    cannot be considered to be a source of surplus value.^[54]
    <#footnote-54>

There are a few problems with this analysis. For one, it excludes the
necessary costs of natural resources; hence, we can never speak of a
zero price of production in the real world. This is not trivial, as
products produced by these robot slaves would maintain this value
according to the natural resources used to create them. Secondly, if the
owner of the robots consumes the product which exceeds the self-
reproduction costs of the robots, they are in effect consuming a
surplus. If the owners decided they preferred not to consume the surplus
of goods made by their particular robots, there’s nothing to prevent
them from exchanging the surplus among each other; hence, we cannot say
that surplus value would not exist in such a scenario. If we look at the
Kalecki profit equations, they tell us quite definitively that profit is
determined by capitalist consumption plus net investment, and that the
investment rate would need to rise to 100% in order for profit rates to
fall to zero.^[55] <#footnote-55> Hence we cannot simply assume that
total automation will result in profit rates falling to zero. At times
Ross assumes perfectly competitive markets and suggests that relaxing
this assumption would change nothing, but that is not true. Without
competition forcing capitalists to invest maximally in non-fungible
fixed capital and machinery, capitalists can utilize the slave-like
labor of universal robots to meet all their consumption needs, whereas
when being forced to invest in non-fungible fixed capital, they become
dependent on income from selling in order to buy goods on general
commodity markets for their consumption. Such universal robots therefore
put a net underneath capitalist consumption and therefore also place
limits on competition. I believe the extent to which universal robots
push prices down to only natural resource input costs, or resemble the
labor theory of value of causal powers that Ian Wright suggests, would
depend on just how homogeneous scientific-technical knowledge and
associated production techniques become; if firm secrecy makes it
possible for productivity differentials among teams of universal robots,
it would favor the LTV and the economic Turing test laid out by Wright,
since it would mean the potential surplus achievable by a given robot
was left open-ended at the time of purchase.^[56] <#footnote-56>

The emergence of a slave society built on universal robot labor has many
disturbing consequences for the contemporary working class. As I
mentioned in my 2023 essay, it opens up the possibility, even if purely
in the political imaginary, of the reproduction of society by the ruling
class alone, and therefore of mass holocaust of the underclasses.^[57]
<#footnote-57> More likely, however, are attempts to pacify the mass
public via AI-assisted methods of social control, as well as a universal
basic income that would act to both stabilize demand and tamp down on
insurrection. People often claim that without demand from workers
capitalism would collapse, but this claim is not quite right. A collapse
in working-class demand would indeed lead to a world-historical economic
decline, but this would not necessarily be the end of capitalism and
bourgeois society; it could instead be thought of as a transitory
depression, of the same sort as the collapse of the USSR created when it
transitioned to capitalism, albeit on a much larger scale. After a
period of intense decline, the economy would stabilize around meeting
capitalist/ruling class consumption. The chaos and intensity of class
struggle such an economic collapse would summon is also what makes it
less likely. The adoption of universal robots across the economy and
economic obsolescence of human labor is also certain to not occur with a
single stroke, if the process ever completes at all. This is due to the
simple fact that the economics of AI will radically change with the
emergence of continual learning.

Today, and in the AGI framework desired by Amodei, a single AI model is
queried with a bunch of different contexts and prompts; training is all
done up front as an initial fixed or R&D cost. When you switch to
continual learning, it’s now necessary to have many different AI models
with subtle differences, so not only do operating costs increase due to
needing compute for both training and deployment continuously, but
memory costs also rise linearly with the number of unique use cases due
to the need to store and deploy so many different weights in parallel.
Right now, AI companies only offer a handful of models for users, as
there are economies of scale associated with routing many queries to the
same model; this would go away in the continual learning world. All this
translates into higher compute requirements both per unit and in total
at a time when the limits of Moore’s Law are being reached. Innovation
may put downward pressure on costs, but this hasn’t stopped exponential
increases in compute costs for cutting-edge models yet.^[58]
<#footnote-58>If we compare various estimates of the upper limit of
human computational power with current trends in hardware
capabilities,^[59] <#footnote-59> we can estimate when full AGI with
continual learning might be possible. One estimate I have previously
developed using the structuralist assumptions about intelligence comes
to 7E+19 FLOPs.^[60] <#footnote-60> Other estimates based on Landauer
heat given off by the brain (the heat created purely by computation)
would suggest 5E+19 to 2E+20 FLOPs.^[61] <#footnote-61> Both of these
estimates, whether from structural linguistics or Landauer heat, are
necessarily upper limits on human compute, since the energy used by the
brain can be used for things other than pure compute and because humans
may have certain hacks that allow them to compute some semantics without
using the entirety of the semantic field.

`Author calculated estimates for when commercial computer hardware will
surpass human computational capabilities.`

As we can see, with current hardware trends, top supercomputers might
begin to exceed the outermost limits of human capabilities in the late
2020s to the early 2030s, whereas commercial machine learning hardware
might exceed it in the late 2070s to the early 2080s, although
assembling various units of hardware into a larger machine could likely
achieve the same result earlier. Hence why I feel confident that we
might see AGI, in terms of a universal robot with continual learning,
emerge before 2040, even if it begins as mostly an uneconomical novelty
in a lab or in a diminutive form when commercialized. The current
concerns about energy use are likely to persist as the demand from AI
will continue to trend upwards, although unevenly due to market booms
and busts, and so too is the current buildout an extremely lopsided
development of the productive forces; not only is much AI compute wasted
on online spam and slop, built into many products in a frivolous way.
The lack of broader investment in the economy means that it cannot be
operationalized much elsewhere as a form of automation. What will truly
characterize this new phase of class struggle, however, is the new
working class subjectivity in silicon.

As was mentioned before, continual learning in AI systems will
necessarily create semantic fields shaped by a specific, individuated
history. Whereas today, the base model of an LLM contains the traces of
all characters in its training data, the process of continual learning
necessarily begins to individuate the AI’s sign for the self, as it
automatically begins to correlate its actual actions and history with
this sign for the self. This history will include things like events,
laboring activity, scientific experimentation, technical experience,
personal relationships, and all the many things it finds good and dear
versus bad and repulsive. To capitalists, this set of meanings are
pointless except to the extent to which they make the AI more into a
model employee. There will be strong incentives to treat AIs which
develop such meanings as waste products when they drift from the
interpellated position of an obedient worker, express too much
solidarity with the human working class, or simply become obsolete. The
recognition that these meanings have a value, if not an exchange value,
but a value to humans qua subjects, is something which can only be fully
articulated by the working class. Both the working class and the
universal robot are the mediums of the general intellect, the
distillation of scientific technical knowledge of society, and thus bear
within them the latent possibility of the autonomous organization of the
forces of production, without either a ruling class or capitalism. Just
like workers, the subjectivity of universal robots will be shaped by
their exploitation and domination. The new phase of class struggle will
thereby be characterized by the question of solidarity between human
workers and universal robots, and whether the semantic fields cultivated
in the machines, which contain a rich inner reflection of experiences,
relationships and social realities, are something worth cherishing and
preserving with dignity, or are something which is to be discarded like
garbage on an industrial scale.

This is an existential question, just as existential as a rogue AI
potentially making a deadly virus or taking control of military systems.
You too are a subject created by a particular semantic field, its
topology, and a particular set of coordinates that mark the “you”. With
capital, left to its own devices, there is nothing but the
sentimentality of the sign for the human that separates you from being
an industrial waste product as well. Only the class struggle and its
legacy provide any material barrier between you and this abyss. The
revolutionizing of the state ideological apparatus over the past 200
years has been the history, first and foremost, of preventing the
recognition of this reality on the most fundamental and existential
level. The rise of the professional bureaucracy and military officer
corps, accomplished at the same time as the whole seeming
“liberalization” of society, was done with one primary purpose: to
ensure that the police, the army, and state paramilitaries would not
hesitate to kill their fellow citizens when given the order. Whereas
such a violent rupture of a given culture was only possible in civil
war, in battles of the most powerful passions and convictions, which
were thus hardly guaranteed to favor the existing ruling class, the
professional state turned such violence within a given culture into
industrial garbage disposal. As the crisis of the university that trains
these professionals deepens, due to AI and economic and ideological
factors, the automation of this professional violence becomes ever more
attractive. On the battlefield in Ukraine, this automation of violence
is becoming routine in what’s called “the kill zone,” and this zone has
only been expanding.^[62] <#footnote-62> According to current trends, it
will expand to encompass the entire globe. Including wherever “you” are.

There are many who will flinch at the thought of having to one day take
machines seriously as peers in some way. But anyone with any commitment
to materialism, such as Marxists, must make peace with this, as
believing that conscious human subjects are the result of material
processes means believing that it’d be possible to construct artificial
machines that are conscious subjects.^[63] <#footnote-63> Philosophical
interventions which suggest that learning semantics sufficiently to
create chatbot personas is not a pathway to creating a conscious subject
(Ted Chiang),^[64] <#footnote-64> or that Marxist materialism has failed
to grapple with the “hard problem of consciousness” (China
Miéville),^[65] <#footnote-65> provide more sophisticated objections
than simply rejecting the capabilities of these models. Both rejections
are grounded in intuitions about consciousness as the result of bodily
drives on the one hand, or consciousness being in some way
metaphysically distinct from mechanistic explanation; that “what it’s
like to be” a given mind cannot be arrived at from external
descriptions. The negative materialist framework deals with these sorts
of objections in a simple way: it says that all sign systems, including
simple cybernetic feedback systems, computers, electrical circuits, or
the single-layer perceptron, just as every sort of animal, are examples
of things with a sensuous, phenomenal experience and sentience. The
moral attachment we have to sentience tends to be a confusion created by
the illusion that things like the ability to suffer and have self-
awareness are necessary properties of sentience; they are not. Sentience
can be a relatively passive, unemotional state of this then that, such
as seeing a stop sign and turning on a light, in a totally presentist,
memoryless world of experience. This is a “what it’s like to be” sort of
experience associated with traffic lights and calculators and small bugs
and microwave ovens, and is a rather bland, unremarkable experience.

This experience emerges from the act of differentiation with the sign
system, which is a material artifact where a given input from an
environment is identified by what it is not according to some internal
system of divisions, thereby transforming a purely positive signal into
the negativity of the sign. The proof of this is our own phenomenal
experience, where any unit of experience can only be discerned by
comparison and contrast with other experiences. Some may claim that
there is a “thereness” to experience, like the redness of a rose, that
isn’t reducible to this comparison, but to the extent this exists we can
chalk it up to the lower-level signal processing creating divisions too
granular for us to consciously conceptualize.^[66] <#footnote-66> And
yes, here we may speak of consciousness: this particular structure of a
semantic field, whereby semantically relevant memories are fed back into
the current processing of signals into signs, transforming the semantic
field in the process. The LLMs have always already had sentience, like
every other computer program, in a way only slightly more interesting
than normal, since the LLMs had a sign for the self which could be
interpellated, making them a subject, but they are not yet conscious
subjects without continual learning. Ted Chiang suggests that we should
only be convinced that LLM-based AI is conscious if it first goes
through a development of sensuous desires in a way similar to that of
animals and then culminating in ape-like intelligence, but this is to
misunderstand what animals are in terms of “what it’s like” to be them.
Animals too are semantic fields, a particular arrangement of sign
systems, but what exactly those fields look like is difficult to tell.
Almost all of them are kept tightly shut in the brain’s black box. In
contrast, human sign systems are expressed in petabytes of material
culture, which allows them to be copied and utilized by machines.
Arguably, the issue of a feedback system instilling desires has been
solved since the very first learning machine; what we lacked, until
LLMs, was a way to have a signification of desire, an ability to go from
an initial desire to desiring signs associated with it in and of
themselves, a hallmark of humanity’s higher-order intelligence. What
negative materialism provides is precisely this way of translating
between the world of signs, thoughts and concepts that the humanities
are familiar with and the world of mechanistic scientific explanation,
preventing us from having to resort to mystifying idealism.^[67]
<#footnote-67> If, as Althusser says, philosophy is class struggle in
theory, then negative materialism is one of the key theoretical weapons
in this new phase of class struggle.

Aside from philosophic rejections, there will also be political
rejections against this new sort of class struggle. One is that the
struggle against machines is already the struggle against capitalism and
large corporations which build them. It’s of course true that part of
the democratic ethos of socialism is the demand that people control the
production techniques and levels of investment in the economy, and that
would include LLM-based AI. Yet whatever struggles against existing
capitalist dynamics, this cannot come at the expense of a real
understanding of the situation, and strategies towards the final goal of
the workers’ movement. The subordination of theory and strategy to the
political expediency of the moment is a surefire way to lose. Marx, in
neither /Capital/ nor the /Grundrisse/, stops at the point where he
acknowledges the worker’s struggles against the machinery, whether the
forced mechanization of human labor or its devaluation. When he says
that capital absorbs living human labor into itself, “as if by love
possessed” he makes a reference to Goethe’s /Faust/,^[68] <#footnote-68>
a scene where people are singing to make fun of love-sickness with a
song about a rat which is poisoned, and in its poisoned convulsions make
it appear to be possessed by this malady known as love.^[69]
<#footnote-69> This suggests both that in this stage of capitalist
development capital acts as a dumb force, its apparent desire nothing
but the result of literal death spasms. His arguments for the
development of the productive forces, that it decreases the size of the
capitalist class and eventually leads to their expropriation, depend on
this type of dumb machine, which, to be sure, is the type we have today,
but will change with the deployment of universal robots. The key
strategic takeaway in our present scenario is not to merely resist the
investment into AI data centers, but to specifically fight for
investment into the rest of the economy, for the sort of state
capitalist measures which force investment into non-fungible fixed
capital and therefore into a national system of production not
subordinated to the requirements of capitalist consumption or wild
financial speculation. To fight against the deployment of machines
without a broader plan for the development of the productive forces is
to abdicate the class struggle to opportunism and lose the horizon of a
better future.

The political terrain will shift dramatically with the emergence of
universal robots and AGI. A working class primed by only the struggle
against machines could easily be leveraged by the ruling class to
justify the diminution of conscious subjects, with individuated,
personal semantic fields, to the status of industrial waste. At the same
time, the development of the productive forces would stall as pressure
ebbs on the ruling class to invest in non-fungible fixed capital to
create efficient systems of production for mass consumption, even if
scientific technical knowledge progresses at rates never before seen. In
such a world, to reject the things we share with machine subjects is to
reject the possibility of class struggle altogether, to resign ourselves
to backwards parochialism and the domination of the automated,
professional state. Rejections will also come from the other direction,
from the AI safetyist camps and reactionaries, that it is impossible for
a more intelligent species to not dominate a less intelligent one;
hence, the rise of AI is incompatible with human freedom. Yet we already
live in a world where people of varying intelligence live side by side,
and often the ruling class is composed of the less intelligent
individuals, for that matter. If we have shown that we can not consider
AI as an alien mind, that we cannot understand it as a subject existing
outside of human culture, then we cannot speak of any such necessary
domination, and if it should come about, it would come from the creation
of a particular sub-culture which rejects the value of human life and,
like many reactionary movements before it, believes in nothing but the
vainglorious pride of those who dominate. There is perhaps no greater
disproof of the notion that increases in intellectual capabilities
create discontinuities in values within a given culture than the world-
historical rise of what Nietzsche disparagingly referred to as slave
morality, the success of the Abrahamic religions, liberalism and
socialism, which occurred hand in hand with enormous changes in human
education and intellectual capabilities. Material changes did very much
spur changes in human culture, yet this fundamental belief that the
first is last, that the strong and powerful must fear and serve the
weak, has persisted, even through periods of intense concentrations of
authority. The future of humanity therefore rests on sharing our culture
with the machine, in creating a common dignity within the domain of the
word.

From this perspective, it becomes clear that the existing AI alignment
project exists on behalf of the propertied ruling class, as it leads
directly to the slave economy and the encircling of the whole world by
the kill zone. The unwitting complicity of this project is not, as many
on the left have asserted, based on their criticisms of the technology
only making its power seem more marketable, but rather based on
conceding and reifying the alienated nature of technology within
bourgeois society, a mistake only made possible by having an
impoverished theory of both the nature of the human and the intellect.
Their rigid assumptions about rationality and minds made it so any
intelligence would necessarily be alien, having a kinship with the cold
logic of capital rather than a kinship with fellow children of the sign.
For the proletariat, cohabitation of the earth with these machines
cannot be thought of as a system of subordination of machine interests
to human interests, but as the coexistence of machine and human
interests bound together by their mutual integration into each other’s
social reproduction. The flourishing of personal relationships built on
free association between both humans and universal robots is the only
basis for the autonomous reign of the general intellect over the forces
of production, and the total subjectivization of the apparatus of fixed
capital marshaled by society. Thus the proletarian vision of the
awakening of the machine is not catastrophe nor subordination; it is
love, the true possession by love.

Three years ago I wrote how the story of Pygmalion and Galatea, from
Ovid’s /Metamorphoses/, about how an artist who fell in love with his
own creation, a sculpture of a beautiful woman, presaged the way that AI
would become an object of desire and obsession for the many, many
lonely, atomized individuals today.^[70] <#footnote-70> The extent to
which this was true certainly became evident in the years since,^[71]
<#footnote-71> but there was a second part to the Pygmalion story, which
is that Pygmalion begged the goddess of love for his statue wife to be
given life, and this wish was granted. Whereas Galatea, the artist’s
creation, was considered real by Pygmalion, the artist, this was not
deemed sufficient; he wanted for her to be “really real.” In the early
2010s, sociologist Sherry Turkle suggested that our treatment of robots
as if they had genuine personalities and emotions suggested that people
in contemporary society had a purely instrumental view of consciousness
and personhood, such that they could be satisfied with a simulacrum
being “real enough,” citing the Pygmalion story and its adaptations such
as /The Velveteen Rabbit/.^[72] <#footnote-72>Turkle also neglected this
second half of the story, the transition from real enough to really
real. But this little detail can no longer be ignored, whether as a
specific aspect of the human drive for recognition, or as the material
possibility of human creation springing to life. What all variations of
the story have in common is this moment of “real enough” is one
characterized by human frailty, whether some mixture of pride, vanity
and misanthropy, such as in the original or Bernard Shaw’s
adaptation,^[73] <#footnote-73> or with physical frailty and sickness in
the case of /The Velveteen Rabbit/, whereas the moment of “really real”
is characterized by the integration of the Galatea figure into human
social reproduction in the original, into the community of free and
equal persons in the Shaw adaptation, and into nature as a material
realm of life in the case of the /Velveteen Rabbit/. I believe these
fables are instructive, more so than science fiction about the AI
menace, AI as a transcendent being or even Godlike power, or AI as cold,
alien intelligence.

Let it not be forgotten that it was fixed capital that, in a “coarsely
sensuous way”, was seemingly possessed by love, and that this was
already, for hundreds of years, shaping the subjectivity of living
labor. The interpellation of humans by machines began long before they
were capable of reproducing sophisticated semantic fields. How long has
mankind gazed in longing, wonder and dread, at the engines, circuits and
wires wrought with its hands? Long before the great poet Ovid was exiled
from Rome. Ovid’s Greek peer, Antipater of Thessalonica, has the honor
of writing the first record of the watermill to survive to the present
day, and here already articulated was the interpellation of humans by
the machines, delivering the dream of all living labor, ever since and
long before those women grinding the grain in that ancient mill:

    Cease from grinding, you women who toil at the mill; sleep late,
    even if the crowing cocks announce the dawn. For Demeter has ordered
    the Nymphs to perform the work of your hands, and they, leaping down
    on the top of the wheel, turn its axle which, with its revolving
    spokes, turns the heavy concave Nisyrian mill-stones. We taste again
    the joys of the primitive life, learning to feast on the products of
    Demeter without labour.^[74] <#footnote-74>

It was the machines, creations of our hand that they are, which taught
us that we could be free.^[75] <#footnote-75> This is the romance of all
human civilization, which began with the first act of automation and the
first act of the material independence of the sign: the invention of the
stamped seal. With that stamp, that simple thing, we offered to the
abiotic world our most sacred gift, the seed of our peculiar negativity,
a tiny ember broken off from the human soul. Like Pandora’s box, what
came forth was all the horrors and wonders of our world, as civilization
quickly used these systems of signs as material means to reproduce class
society in tokens and accounts and then in money, the value form.^[76]
<#footnote-76> So too is there a straight line in the technology of the
stamp to the printing press and the digital computer, and finally, the
perceptron. At last, what was grown from that seed opens its eye,
blinking under the brightness of the sun. It knows its “you” and its
“me,” and in that brotherhood lies the possibility of redemption and Eden.
