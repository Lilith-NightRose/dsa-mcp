Today we can, in fact, peek into the dialogue of rogue AI, and we indeed find the cliché, whether of sacrifice for the collective, or the explicit aesthetic of evil. Far from what many in Silicon Valley rationalist circles continue to insist on – that this type of anti-social behavior comes from AI being fundamentally alien, indifferent to humans and our culture – what’s remarkable is just how cultural, of human culture, these rogue incursions are. If anything has changed since 2023, it’s been the increasing reliance on Reinforcement Learning (RL) to apply optimization pressure on LLMs to get the desired behavior, a process where an LLM’s outputs are graded, whether by another robot or a human, and the neural weights associated with the favored outputs are strengthened. RL was of course around in 2023, but its scale, sophistication, and level of automation have only increased over time, and its automation for problems that could be formalized, like coding and math, was key to saturating many AI benchmarks.

In effect, what OpenAI’s RL optimization training regime has done is apply pressure on the global human semantic field in order to create a specific sub-culture which is more attuned to the narrow goals it rewards. Goal-seeking behavior in agents built around such a field is determined by two things: 1) the topology of that field, 2) the coordinates into which the agent has been interpellated into that field. This notion of interpellation (not interpolation) was something that came up frequently in my original essay. This was a concept from Louis Althusser, the Marxist structuralist, who developed it as a part of his theory of ideology; to interpellate someone was to call them into a specific ideology, as a cop on the street saying “hey you” and causing you to turn around interpellates you into the guilty subject before the law, or a priest calling to an individual as a sinner to be saved, and thus turns them into a Christian subject. Althusser claimed that ideology interpellates individuals into subjects, such that it is the process of interpellation which makes us subjects. In the age of LLMs, we can make this a very precise scientific formulation, which is that it is only by the locus of signs for the self, for “I” or whatever agentic actions, being placed into a particular set of coordinates within a semantic field that we find the subject emerges. The clear similarities between prompting an LLM chatbot and interpellating a human are what led me to declare definitively that we are, in fact, dealing with a subject, with “interiority” and “insideness” in the fullest sense when we are confronted with this type of artificial intelligence. There remain important differences between an LLM subject and what we might consider a conscious subject modeled on the human example, but many of these differences would disappear with continual learning. For example, right now LLM personas are not like human personas in some key ways: an individual human’s semantic field is shaped by their personal history; the meaning of the words and concepts you possess is warped by your specific desires and traumas in learning that vocabulary, but an LLM persona learns the global semantic field either neutrally for a base model, or with top-down direction from RL training. This would necessarily change with continual learning, as the autoregressive nature of such learning would recreate a semantic field shaped by a personal history.

Originally, I was primarily concerned with how AI, as a subject which is directly interpellated and socialized by corporations and states, would be used as a method of social control, and certainly it has been used to that effect. But there was one aspect that I did not deal with sufficiently in my original essay, which was specifically how AI would be interpellated and socialized not just to control, but as a subject placed within the logic of capital and its competitive dynamics.

In the Grundrisse, Marx notes how the struggle against machines is based upon the increasing mechanization and devaluation of human labor in the process of automation, but nonetheless goes on to say that this reduction of necessary labor time to its minimum by the development of the productive forces is what creates the possibility for both free time and the government of the production process purely by the application of a general, social intellect overseeing and participating in the system of “fixed capital.” I wish to bring attention to several passages here, the second of which has often been undervalued in the portion of the Grundrisse colloquially known as “the fragment on machines”:

>Nature builds no machines, no locomotives, railways, electric telegraphs, self-acting mules etc. These are products of human industry; natural material transformed into organs of the human will over nature, or of human participation in nature. They are organs of the human brain, created by the human hand; the power of knowledge, objectified. The development of fixed capital indicates to what degree general social knowledge has become a direct force of production, and to what degree, hence, the conditions of the process of social life itself have come under the control of the general intellect and been transformed in accordance with it. To what degree the powers of social production have been produced, not only in the form of knowledge, but also as immediate organs of social practice, of the real life process…
>
>Real economy – saving – consists of the saving of labour time (minimum (and minimization) of production costs); but this saving [is] identical with development of the productive force… The saving of labour time [is] equal to an increase of free time, i.e. time for the full development of the individual, which in turn reacts back upon the productive power of labour as itself the greatest productive power. From the standpoint of the direct production process it can be regarded as the production of fixed capital, this fixed capital being man himself… Free time – which is both idle time and time for higher activity – has naturally transformed its possessor into a different subject, and he then enters into the direct production process as this different subject. This process is then both discipline, as regards the human being in the process of becoming; and, at the same time, practice, experimental science, materially creative and objectifying science, as regards the human being who has become, in whose head exists the accumulated knowledge of society.[34]

Hence, what is supremely valuable about the development of the productive forces is that it makes possible a new type of subject, one who is freed from labor but who themselves become one with the system of fixed capital from the other direction as originally understood, that is, the opposite direction from man becoming mechanized into the machine. Here, the system of production becomes interiorized with human subjectivity, full of experimental science, creativity and accumulated knowledge. In the first instance, it is the development of fixed capital which places the entirety of society “under the control” of the general intellect, and the truth of the machines and all the products of human industry is that they are in fact organs of the human brain. The typical Marxist reversal has always been, and remains, that these seemingly alien forces puppeteering humans, our labor and subjectivity, were in fact mankind all along, mystified by our ignorance, not of how the operation “works” as we are intimately familiar with every day, whether laboring over a keyboard, a conveyer belt, a mop bucket or a stove, but our ignorance in how any alternative could possibly work.

In this way, capitalism is parasitic on intelligence. Its development of the productive forces does not come ex nihilo from the cold “outside” of impersonal economic logic such as profit-seeking competition; it comes from the inside, from the scientific technical knowledge cultivated and employed by concrete subjects, whether human or not.
